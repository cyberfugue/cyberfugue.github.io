No one would have believed

In the last years of the nineteenth century, a cylinder falls on Horsell Common, near Woking. The crowd gathers at its pit; the lid unscrews; something looks out. Within a week the Martians’ tripods are wading the Thames, the Black Smoke lies over the Home Counties, and London is emptying northward along every road. Wells tells it as a man who lived through it, and his brother who got away, writing six years after.

“No one would have believed in the last years of the nineteenth century that this world was being watched keenly and closely by intelligences greater than man’s and yet as mortal as his own.”Book One, Chapter I
I

The war, drawn as you read it

Beside the text the war is drawn live, in pen, wash and hatching, in the manner of Alvim Corrêa’s plates: the pit at sunset as the lid falls, the Heat-Ray sweeping the common, the storm on the Maybury road, the river at Shepperton, the Black Smoke, the Thunder Child, the red weed, dead London. What the text tells happens there, at its word: read by eye, as the paragraph comes under it; listened to, as the word is spoken. Drag it to look about.

The dateline above it keeps the day and the hour, and the page darkens with the night in the story.

II

“And this Thing I saw!”

The storm breaks over the hill beyond Pyrford church, and in a flash of lightning a fighting-machine strides out over the pines. The night from the study window, with the country burning towards Woking. The tripods at Shepperton wading out of the smoke. More than seventy scenes, each cued to the words that tell it.

III

Read aloud, word by word

Two complete LibriVox readings are inside the app: johnnyenglish and Cori Samuel. Every word of the book is timed to each, so the word being spoken is lit, the page follows the voice, and the drawing and the sound follow it too. Change readers at the same place; tap any paragraph to hear it from there. It plays with the screen locked, with a sleep timer and a choice of speeds.

Watch turns the drawing full size, with the words beneath it as they are read.

IV

Alvim Corrêa’s plates

The thirty-three plates of the Brussels edition of 1906, drawn by Henrique Alvim Corrêa for five hundred signed copies, set after the passages they show, with his captions. Wells is said to have said that Corrêa did more for his work with his brush than he had done with his pen. Tap one to see it full size, and swipe between them.

V

The map of the war, and its days

A map of Surrey, Middlesex and the Thames from the book, on the real ground: where each cylinder fell, where the Martians went, the narrator’s way and his brother’s flight to the Essex coast, and the Black Smoke spreading. Drag the days from the first falling star to the dead machines on Primrose Hill; what you have read is drawn, the rest is waiting.

VI

Moments to do

Where the book gives you something to see for yourself, you can: look through Ogilvy’s telescope at Mars; watch the end of the cylinder turn, and the thin circle of bright metal widen; peer through the slit in the wall of the ruined house at Sheen; keep still in the coal cellar as the tentacle comes feeling for you; follow the cry of “Ulla” across dead London; and turn about the specimen in spirits at the Natural History Museum, touching its parts.

VII

On iPhone Duo, a broadsheet

Open the phone and the reading becomes a newspaper’s front page: the masthead across the top with the day, the chapter and the place, the text in a newspaper’s column, and the war as the tall engraving beside it. Open it while reading and the war spreads across the whole page before the paper settles beside it; open it while the book is read aloud and the war fills the screen with the words beneath. Fold it, and the page is back at the same word.

About this edition

The War of the Worlds was serialised in Pearson’s Magazine in 1897 and published by William Heinemann in 1898; the text is in the public domain. Henrique Alvim Corrêa’s plates are from the French translation published in Brussels by L. Vandamme in 1906, from Duke University’s copy at the Internet Archive. The readings by johnnyenglish and Cori Samuel are LibriVox’s, in the public domain. The drawn war, the sound and music (the heath at night, the hammering in the pit, the Heat-Ray’s hum, the guns, the Thames, the cry of “Ulla”), the map and the moments were made for this edition, from the book.

Everything is inside the app and works with no connection. The app collects no data. Headphones are best.

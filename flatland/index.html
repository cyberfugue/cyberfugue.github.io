A world of two dimensions

In 1884 a London headmaster wrote the memoir of A Square, a gentleman of a world with only two dimensions, where women are Straight Lines, priests are Circles, and nobody can see anything but a line. Then a Sphere passes through his plane, and lifts him out of it. Flatland has been read ever since as a satire on Victorian society and as the best way into the idea of a fourth dimension. This edition lets you go there.

“I call our world Flatland, not because we call it so, but to make its nature clearer to you, my happy readers, who are privileged to live in Space.”
1D

Lineland

A single beam of light, whose people can never pass one another. Walk along it and hear a tone rise as your neighbours draw near, and meet the King who can see nothing but a Point, with Abbott’s own figure of his kingdom drawn in light beside the words.

2D

Flatland

Walk as A Square among Triangles, Pentagons and Hexagons, in the fog that lets its people tell one another apart. A strip at the top shows all that A Square can see: one line, brighter where things are near. Tilt the phone up toward you and your own eye comes down to the edge of the plane, as Abbott asks, until you see as he does. Walk into a Triangle and feel its angle prick.

3D

A Stranger from Space

Look down on Flatland from Spaceland, and lower the Sphere through the plane. Its circle grows from a point, swells, shrinks and vanishes, and a window shows what A Square sees of it from inside the plane. Turn the phone to look around, or set Flatland down on the table in front of you, through the camera.

4D

The fourth dimension

In Hyperland, turn a tesseract or a hypersphere through the fourth dimension, and slide a three-dimensional slice through it, as the Sphere slid through Flatland. Its shadow and the line where it meets Flatland are drawn with it. Or build one yourself, as A Square argues it: drag a Point into a Line, a Square, a Cube, and a Tesseract with sixteen terminal points.

Read aloud, word by word

Ruth Golding’s LibriVox reading of the whole book is inside the app. Each word is lit as she says it, and the worlds follow her voice: to Lineland when A Square dreams of it, to Spaceland when the Sphere lifts him out, and to Pointland, humming alone. When the Sphere speaks, its words come from above your head. Read along, follow captions, or just listen. It plays with the screen locked.

Abbott’s figures, redrawn in light

The complete book: all twenty-two sections, the title page and dedication, and the editor’s preface to the second edition. Abbott’s eleven figures are redrawn as luminous line art, worked out from the geometry, and draw themselves in as you reach them; the 1884 originals are a tap away.
